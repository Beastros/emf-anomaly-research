// 01 -- Event overview

What happened that night

On the evening of March 13, 1997, something crossed the sky above the American Southwest. Starting near Henderson, Nevada just before 8pm local time, and traveling southeast through Arizona over the next two and a half hours, it was observed by over a thousand independent witnesses -- including airline pilots, police officers, military personnel, and the governor of Arizona himself.

It made no sound. It appeared as a massive V-shaped formation of lights estimated by multiple credentialed witnesses to span over a mile across. It blocked out the stars as it passed overhead. And near Tucson, it was gone.

The US Air Force's official explanation: flares dropped by A-10 Warthog aircraft during a training exercise. That explanation has significant problems -- four of eight tracked speed segments exceeded the A-10's maximum, A-10s appear on radar, and flares don't disturb magnetometers in Colorado.

On the methodology: everything in this report comes from publicly available government data -- USGS magnetometer archives, GFZ Potsdam geomagnetic indices, and NOAA NEXRAD radar records. Every finding can be independently reproduced. Source code at github.com/Beastros/emf-anomaly-research.

// 02 -- The two-station discovery

One thousand kilometers apart. Moving in lockstep.

The most striking finding isn't the spike in Tucson. It's what happened simultaneously 1,013 kilometers away in Boulder, Colorado.

We compared the Tucson magnetometer against the Boulder magnetometer minute-by-minute during the event. Two completely independent government instruments in different states with their own atomic clocks. Their D-field readings correlated at 0.9704 out of 1.0 for the entire 70-minute window. The time lag between them: one minute -- essentially simultaneous. Implied propagation speed: over 60,000 km/h.

That's not a physical object moving through air. It means whatever caused this was either large enough to affect both states at once, or generated a field that propagated at near-electromagnetic speeds -- close to the speed of light.

Figure 1. Multi-station magnetometer wavefront analysis. Second panel: TUC (red) and BOU (green) D-field -- two lines in near-perfect unison across 1,013 km. Red vertical line = 03:32 UTC, last sighting, moment of the 6.333-sigma spike. No eyewitness timestamps used in this calculation.

This is the key finding. Two independent government instruments in different states showing 0.9704 correlation during a specific 70-minute window on a geomagnetically quiet night. Stations in Alaska, Hawaii, and Virginia showed nothing -- confirming this was localized to the Arizona-Colorado corridor, not a solar event.

// 05 -- Honest limits

What we can and cannot conclude

The findings are real and reproducible. But rigorous analysis requires stating what the data doesn't prove.

Classified military activity. The US military operates classified aircraft and electronic warfare systems. We cannot access classified flight records. This is the most credible conventional explanation that survives the full dataset -- though it struggles to account for simultaneous 0.97 correlation across 1,013 km.

Unusual ionospheric event. Ionospheric disturbances can affect magnetometers at multiple stations. However these are associated with solar activity -- which was confirmed absent. A spontaneous event of this scale during perfectly quiet conditions coinciding exactly with the sighting window would itself need explaining.

What the data clearly shows: the A-10 flare explanation is inconsistent with the sensor record. A-10s appear on radar. They don't disturb magnetometers in Colorado. The instruments say something happened that the official explanation doesn't account for.
