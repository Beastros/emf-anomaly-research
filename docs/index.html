On March 13, 1997, thousands of people across Arizona watched a massive formation of lights move silently across the state for over two hours. It's one of the most widely witnessed UAP events in American history, reported by police officers, military veterans, and then-Governor Fife Symington, who later said publicly that what he saw was not flares.

The debate has mostly stayed in the realm of eyewitness accounts. We wanted to add a different layer: what did the instruments say? Geomagnetic sensors, Doppler radar, space weather indices — all publicly archived, all timestamped. If something with unusual electromagnetic or physical properties moved through Arizona that night, it had the opportunity to leave a mark in the data.

It did.

How the analysis works

We built an open-source sniffer that cross-correlates independent public sensor streams against documented event timelines. The core logic is straightforward: any single sensor can produce a false spike. But when multiple unrelated instruments — a magnetometer in Tucson, a Doppler radar archive, a space weather station — flag the same short window simultaneously, those instruments have no shared noise source. That convergence is physically meaningful regardless of cause.

Nine documented witness points across Arizona were used to reconstruct the event track — a modeled path stretching over 800 km across the state. That geometry gives us the timestamps and positions to know exactly which sensor windows to care about. Every sensor is then normalized against its own rolling 20-minute baseline, so what we're measuring is departure from local context, not absolute field strength.

Before looking at any results, we checked the Kp index — the standard measure of global geomagnetic disturbance driven by solar activity. On March 13–14, 1997, it was 2.0. That's a quiet night. No solar storm, no auroral activity that could drive magnetometer anomalies across the Southwest. That baseline condition is what makes the subsequent findings meaningful: space weather is off the table as an explanation.

What the sensors recorded

The Tucson (TUC) and Boulder (BOU) magnetometer stations are 1,013 km apart. Both are part of the INTERMAGNET global monitoring network, independently operated, with no shared hardware. During the Phoenix Lights window, the cross-station correlation between TUC and BOU was 0.9704 at a lag of −1 minute — meaning both stations moved in near-perfect synchrony, with TUC registering slightly ahead of BOU.

Two independent instruments over a thousand kilometers apart moved in near-perfect lockstep during the event window. Using only the known station separation and the time-lag between their peaks — no eyewitness timestamps, no assumptions about the event — the implied propagation velocity of whatever affected both stations exceeds 60,000 km/h.

The D-field at TUC peaked at 6.333σ above baseline at 03:32 UTC, four minutes after the last reported sighting. 178 individual magnetometer samples were flagged across the full window. The anomaly doesn't drift slowly across the night — it spikes and resolves, consistent with something passing through rather than a sustained atmospheric condition.

On the radar side, the Phoenix area was inside NEXRAD Doppler coverage on March 13, 1997. The null-return score — a measure of how absent an object is from radar returns despite being in an active coverage zone — came back at 0.928 out of 1.0. The formation was in coverage. The radar saw almost nothing. That result is not explained by clouds or atmospheric ducting; it reads as an object that wasn't returning radar energy the way any conventional aircraft would.

What this analysis does and doesn't claim: The instrument data is real. The anomalies are real. What caused them is not something these sensors can determine. The methodology rules out solar weather as a driver — the Kp gate is the first check and March 13–14, 1997 passes it cleanly. Beyond that, the data documents a pattern. The full methodology, all data sources, and every script used to produce these results are publicly available and independently reproducible.

Phoenix is one event in a larger pattern

The same analysis framework has been run against four other cases: the USS Nimitz encounter (2004), the O'Hare Airport sighting (2006), JAL Flight 1628 (1986), and MH370 (2014). Phoenix, Nimitz, and O'Hare all produce the same electromagnetic signature — cross-station magnetometer correlation above 0.97, near-zero time lag, a field disturbance affecting stations hundreds to thousands of kilometers apart simultaneously. MH370 shows geomagnetic anomalies at exact zero-minute offset from transponder cutoff across three independent stations, a finding not previously reported. The dataset is starting to reveal at least two physically distinct signature types across the five events. Full findings are in the repository.
