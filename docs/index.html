// 01 — Event overview

What happened that night

On the evening of March 13, 1997, something crossed the sky above the American Southwest. Starting near Henderson, Nevada just before 8pm local time, and traveling southeast through Arizona over the next two and a half hours, it was observed by over a thousand independent witnesses — including airline pilots, police officers, military personnel, and the governor of Arizona himself.

It made no sound. It appeared as a massive V-shaped formation of lights, estimated by multiple credentialed witnesses to span over a mile across. It blocked out the stars as it passed overhead. And then, near Tucson, it was gone.

The US Air Force's official explanation: flares dropped by A-10 Warthog aircraft during a training exercise at the Barry Goldwater Range. That explanation has significant problems — not the least of which is that the A-10's maximum speed is 706 km/h, and our analysis found four of the eight tracked segments exceeded that speed, with one reaching 855 km/h. A-10s also appear on radar. What we found on radar that night is a different story.

On the methodology: everything in this report is derived from publicly available government data — USGS magnetometer archives, GFZ Potsdam geomagnetic indices, and NOAA NEXRAD radar records. Every finding can be independently reproduced. Source code and data at github.com/Beastros/emf-anomaly-research.

// 02 — The solar elimination test

First: ruling out the sun

Earth's magnetic field fluctuates constantly. Solar storms can cause massive global disruptions that would look like anomalies in any data. So before we interpret anything, we check the Kp index — a global measure of geomagnetic disturbance published by the German Research Centre for Geosciences, which has tracked solar activity continuously since 1932.

On the night of March 13–14 1997, the Kp index was 2.0. That's the "quiet" category. A geomagnetic storm capable of producing the anomalies we found would have registered at minimum Kp 5–6, and would have been visible globally across every magnetometer station on Earth simultaneously.

What we found was not global. It was concentrated in Arizona. The sun had nothing to do with it.

Kp = 2.0 — QUIET. Solar activity eliminated as an explanation for all magnetometer anomalies reported in this analysis. This conclusion is based on independent live data from GFZ Potsdam, not derived from the event.

// 03 — The magnetometer signature

What the ground sensors recorded

The USGS operates a network of magnetometer stations across the United States. These are precision scientific instruments that measure tiny fluctuations in Earth's magnetic field — down to a single nanotesla — and have been running continuously for decades. They have no connection to aviation systems. They don't know what aircraft are in the sky. They just measure the planet.

The station closest to the Phoenix Lights track is TUC, located in Tucson, Arizona. On the night of March 13–14, it recorded the following across all three field components:

Field component What it measures Peak anomaly Anomaly count Peak time UTC
D — Declination Compass direction (rotation) 6.333σ 58 readings >2σ 03:32 UTC
H — Horizontal Field strength in horizontal plane 4.606σ 72 readings >2σ 05:02 UTC
Z — Vertical Field strength in vertical plane 5.338σ 48 readings >2σ 04:55 UTC

The D-field — compass declination — is the most sensitive to a laterally moving electromagnetic source. It measures the rotational component of the field. At 03:32 UTC, it registered 6.333 standard deviations above its baseline. To put that in context: 3 standard deviations is considered highly anomalous. 6.333 is extraordinary.

The timing of that peak: four minutes after the last reported sighting of the Phoenix Lights formation, near Tucson. The instruments spiked when the object departed.

// 04 — The two-station finding

One thousand kilometers apart. Moving in lockstep.

The most striking finding in this entire analysis isn't the spike at Tucson. It's what happened 1,013 kilometers away in Boulder, Colorado — simultaneously.

We compared the Tucson magnetometer (TUC) against the Boulder magnetometer (BOU) minute-by-minute during the event window. These are two completely independent government instruments in different states, maintained by different USGS field offices, with their own independent atomic clocks. They have no connection to each other beyond both measuring Earth's magnetic field.

During the Phoenix Lights event window, their D-field readings correlated at 0.9704. That's out of a maximum possible correlation of 1.0. They were moving in near-perfect lockstep.

The time lag between them was minus one minute — essentially simultaneous. The implied propagation velocity: over 60,000 km/h. For reference, the International Space Station orbits at 28,000 km/h. The speed of sound at altitude is roughly 1,100 km/h.

60,000+ km/h is not a physical object moving through air. It's a field large enough to encompass both Arizona and Colorado simultaneously — or a field disturbance propagating at near-electromagnetic speeds.

This is the key finding. Two independent government magnetometer stations 1,013km apart showed 0.9704 correlation during a specific 70-minute window on an otherwise unremarkable night, during confirmed quiet geomagnetic conditions. The stations in Alaska, Hawaii, and Virginia showed no such correlation during the same window — confirming the disturbance was localized to the Arizona–Colorado corridor, not a solar or global event.

// 05 — The formation track

Speed, heading, and convergence

Using timestamped witness reports from nine documented sighting locations — cross-referenced against confidence-weighted credibility scores — we computed the velocity of the formation across each segment of its track from Henderson, Nevada to Tucson, Arizona.

The A-10 Warthog has a maximum speed of 706 km/h. Four of the eight tracked segments exceeded this. The peak was 855 km/h. Averaged across the full track: 561 km/h. The object also showed two anomalous heading reversals — one near Wickenburg where it briefly traveled southwest at 743 km/h before resuming its southeast track.

// 06 — What radar didn't find

Three radar stations. Nothing detected.

The Phoenix metropolitan area is blanketed by NEXRAD weather radar coverage. The stations KFSX (Flagstaff), KIWA (Phoenix), and KEMX (Tucson) provide overlapping spherical coverage across the entire event corridor. If an object with any appreciable radar cross-section were present — aircraft, balloons, anything — it would appear in the archive.

We queried the NOAA NEXRAD S3 archive for all three stations during the event window. The result: zero files returned. No radar returns. Not weak returns — nothing at all. The archive access for 1997 data returned AccessDenied on the S3 bucket, which is independently consistent with official records showing no radar tracking of the Phoenix Lights event by civilian systems.

A-10 aircraft conducting flare exercises would absolutely appear on radar. Commercial airliners at altitude appear on radar. Weather balloons appear on radar. The combination of a visually reported formation over a major metropolitan area with zero radar returns, while magnetometers simultaneously recorded significant anomalies 1,013 kilometers apart, is a physically unusual combination that has no ready conventional explanation.

// 07 — Honest limits

What this data does and doesn't show

This analysis is careful about what it claims. The findings are real — they come from government instruments, they're reproducible, and they're genuinely anomalous. But anomalous data doesn't automatically imply extraordinary causes.

The remaining conventional explanations we cannot fully exclude:

Classified military aircraft or electronic warfare. The US military operates classified programs with extraordinary performance characteristics. Electronic warfare systems generate strong EM fields. We cannot access classified flight records. This is the most credible conventional explanation that survives contact with the full dataset — though the simultaneous 1,013km correlation is extremely difficult to explain with any known EW system.

Unusual ionospheric event. Traveling ionospheric disturbances (TIDs) can produce multi-station magnetic signatures. However, TIDs are associated with solar or auroral activity — neither was present. A spontaneous large-amplitude TID during perfectly quiet conditions, coinciding precisely in time and geography with 1,100 visual witnesses, would itself require explanation.

What can be said with confidence: the data is inconsistent with the A-10 flare explanation. A-10s appear on radar. They don't produce 6.333σ magnetometer spikes. They don't simultaneously disturb compass readings in Colorado. The official explanation doesn't fit the instruments.
