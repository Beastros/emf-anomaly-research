// Finding 01 — Formation track

Something crossed 720 km of Arizona that night

Using nine timestamped witness locations, we computed the velocity of the formation across each leg of its track.

The colored lines on the map show speed per segment. Green = below A-10 cruise speed. Amber = within A-10 range. Red = faster than the A-10's maximum. The red circles are "convergence events" — moments where multiple independent sensors flagged anomalies at the same time and place. The bar charts on the right show speed and heading for each segment.

Why this is interesting

The US Air Force's official explanation was A-10 Warthogs dropping flares during a training exercise. The A-10's maximum speed is 706 km/h. Four of eight tracked segments exceeded that — with a peak of 855 km/h. The formation also made an anomalous heading reversal near Wickenburg, briefly traveling southwest at 743 km/h before resuming its southeast track. Note: these velocities are derived from witness timestamps and carry inherent uncertainty — but even with generous margins, several segments are difficult to explain with the A-10 attribution.

// Finding 02 — Two-station magnetometer correlation

Two government instruments 1,013 km apart moved in near-perfect lockstep

This is the most unusual finding. Look at the two lines in the chart below.

A magnetometer measures tiny fluctuations in Earth's magnetic field — essentially a very precise compass. There is one in Tucson, Arizona (TUC) and one in Boulder, Colorado (BOU). They are 1,013 km apart, operated by different USGS field offices, with independent atomic clocks. They have no connection to each other beyond both measuring the planet.

The red line is Tucson. The green line is Boulder. The shaded window is the Phoenix Lights event. Watch what happens to both lines during that window.

Fig 2. Multi-station magnetometer wavefront analysis — 5 panels. Top: all 5 stations normalized D-field over the full day — TUC and BOU visibly track together during the event window. Second panel: TUC (red) vs BOU (green) zoomed — correlation 0.9704 at lag −1 minute, implied velocity >60,000 km/h. No eyewitness timestamps used. Third panel: sigma anomaly scores — only TUC spikes at 03:32 UTC; Alaska, Hawaii, and Virginia stay flat. Bottom: station network map and summary. Red vertical line throughout = 03:32 UTC, last sighting, moment of the 6.333σ spike.
Why this is weird

Their correlation during the event window was 0.9704 out of a maximum of 1.0. The time lag between them was one minute — essentially simultaneous across 1,013 km. The implied propagation velocity: over 60,000 km/h. For reference, the International Space Station orbits at 28,000 km/h.

Crucially: the stations in Alaska, Hawaii, and Virginia showed nothing. If this were a solar storm or global geomagnetic event, all stations would move together. It didn't — it was localized to the Arizona–Colorado corridor. The sun is not the explanation.

// Finding 03 — Terminal anomaly

The magnetic field spiked 6.333σ four minutes after the last sighting

Look at panel 3 of the chart above — the anomaly score graph. Every station is background noise. Then at 03:32 UTC, one station fires.

Sigma (σ) is a measure of how far a reading deviates from its own recent baseline. Scientists generally flag anything above 3σ as highly anomalous. The Tucson magnetometer's D-field (compass declination axis) hit 6.333σ — more than double the threshold for "highly anomalous." This is the largest single reading of the night, on the most directionally sensitive axis. It arrived four minutes after the last reported visual sighting of the formation near Tucson.

The solar elimination test

Before any magnetometer finding means anything, we check the Kp index — a global measure of solar storm activity published by the German Research Centre for Geosciences, running continuously since 1932. On March 13–14 1997, Kp was 2.0 — firmly quiet. A geomagnetic storm capable of producing a 6.333σ reading would have registered at Kp 5–9 and disturbed every station on Earth simultaneously. This did neither. Solar activity is eliminated as a cause.

// Finding 04 — Radar

No civilian agency officially reported tracking it

One important caveat: NEXRAD is a weather radar network — it detects atmospheric returns, not necessarily aircraft. When we queried the NOAA S3 archive for the 1997 event window, all three stations returned AccessDenied. Pre-2008 NEXRAD data is not publicly accessible. We cannot independently verify a null return from raw files — and we're saying that plainly rather than claiming "nothing detected."

What is on the record: no FAA radar facility, no civilian agency, officially reported tracking anything during the Phoenix Lights event. That's the accurate claim. The magnetometer findings stand independently of the radar question.

What this does and doesn't mean

The absence of official civilian radar detection is notable but not conclusive. Military radar records are classified. NEXRAD wasn't designed to track aircraft. The honest position: something was observed by over a thousand people across 720 km of American airspace, and the public record contains no radar tracking of it. Draw your own conclusions from that gap.

// Honest limits

What this data does and doesn't prove

Classified military activity — The US military operates classified aircraft and electronic warfare systems with extraordinary performance characteristics that generate strong EM fields. We cannot access classified flight records. This remains the most credible conventional explanation that survives the full dataset — though the 1,013 km simultaneous correlation is extremely difficult to attribute to any known EW system.
Ionospheric disturbance — Traveling ionospheric disturbances can affect multiple magnetometers. However these are associated with solar activity, which was confirmed absent. A spontaneous large-amplitude TID during Kp 2.0 conditions coinciding exactly with the event window would itself require explanation.
Witness timestamp uncertainty — Velocity calculations come from human-reported times and locations. The speed figures carry margin of error. "855 km/h" is an estimate, not a precision measurement.
What the data does show clearly: The A-10 flare explanation is inconsistent with the magnetometer record. Flares do not produce 6.333σ D-field anomalies at government observatories. They do not simultaneously disturb compass readings in Colorado. The instruments registered something the official explanation does not account for.
